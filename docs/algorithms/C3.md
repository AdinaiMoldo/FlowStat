# C3. Execution engine, including the feasible critical path

Increment: I2 (engine, plain critical path), I4 (feasible). Level 1 is finished (M2, 5 November 2026); level 2 is finished in M5 (5 November 2026).

## Two levels

- **Level 1 (known algorithm, our transformation):** Event-driven DAG scheduler: four join rules (all_success, any_success, all_done, at_least k), bounded parallelism, cancellation, goals.
- **Level 2 (built on the output of level 1):** Feasible critical path: the longest chain of steps whose optimistic-run conditions are jointly satisfiable (C2 and C6); priority of the ready queue.
- **Dependence:** Level 2 ranks ready steps by a chain that level 1 can actually execute; I2 ships the plain longest path as a baseline.
- **Baseline for the benchmark:** FIFO, plain longest path and feasible path: makespan on generated DAGs.

## Problem (level 1)

Run the steps of a plan as soon as they are allowed to, at most `m` at a time, so that the result of
the run (the final state of every step and the outcome) is the same whatever the timing: a quorum
join `at_least: 7` of nine regions must give the same answer when region 3 is slow and when it is
fast. Steps can be skipped by a condition, fail, wait for a person, or break a contract, and the
run must end with an explicit outcome (SUCCEEDED, GOAL_NOT_REACHED, FAILED or CANCELLED), never a
success that is not one (DR-02).

## Why a library does not solve it

Task queues and DAG runners (Celery chains, Airflow, networkx topological generations) run a step
when its upstream steps have succeeded, and they decide the fate of a step at the moment something
happens. Our join rules need the opposite: a step is decided only when all its predecessors are
terminal, with a verdict that depends on the *states* of the predecessors (skipped, failed,
upstream failed, cancelled), a conditional skip that propagates differently through `all_success`
and `all_done`, quorum, a failure policy per step, and a waiting state that releases its worker. The
scheduler also has to be a pure function of events, so that the same code runs on threads in
production and on a virtual clock in 600 random tests.

## What is borrowed (textbook algorithms, named)

- Kahn-style readiness counting: a step becomes a candidate when its count of unfinished predecessors reaches zero.
- List scheduling on `m` identical workers with a priority queue (a binary heap); priority by the longest remaining chain (the critical-path rule of Hu, 1961, in the form of a "level" of a node).
- A discrete-event simulation with a time-ordered queue (for the simulated driver).

## What is ours (the transformation and the code we write)

`engine/scheduler.py` (about 300 lines):

- A state machine with nine states per step and four join verdicts computed from the states of the predecessors (`_join_verdict`). All of `all_success`, `any_success`, `at_least k` and `all_done` answer GO, UPSTREAM_FAILED or SKIPPED; UPSTREAM_FAILED is chosen only when a failure could have mattered.
- Decision at "all predecessors terminal": `when` is evaluated there (its references point to ancestors only), arguments are rendered at dispatch time, `ensures` is checked when the action returns.
- A failure policy per step: `fail` stops the run (running steps get `Cancel`, everything not finished becomes CANCELLED); `continue` only travels through the joins.
- Waiting: the step releases its slot; a `signal` finishes it; a signal for a step that is not waiting is ignored.
- Commands out (`Start`, `Cancel`), events in (`step_done`, `step_failed`, `step_waiting`, `signal`, `cancel`), every transition logged as an event with the time given by the driver.
- Two drivers around the same core: `engine/simulation.py` (virtual clock, seeded order of simultaneous events) and `engine/runner.py` (thread pool; workers only run actions, the scheduler stays single-threaded).

## Adaptation: where the plain textbook version is wrong

- Plain topological execution starts a step "when its predecessors succeeded" and leaves the others pending for ever. A run whose condition skipped a step then never ends. Ours gives every step a terminal state, and the run ends only when all are terminal. Test: `test_false_condition_skips_the_step_and_all_success_followers_but_not_all_done`, and the invariant "every step is terminal at the end" in `test_invariants_of_every_run`.
- Early start of a quorum step (as soon as 7 of 9 succeeded) makes the result depend on timing: a fan-in reads whichever instances happened to be done. Ours waits for all predecessors; the oracle test compares final states for 1, 2, 3 and 100 workers and five seeds. Tests: `test_results_do_not_depend_on_timing_or_on_the_number_of_workers`, `test_quorum_with_two_failing_loads_runs_consolidate`.
- A shortfall of a quorum caused only by skips is a skip, not a failure (`test_at_least_shortfall_from_skips_is_a_skip_not_a_failure`); with failures it is UPSTREAM_FAILED (`test_quorum_with_three_failing_loads_is_upstream_failed`). The textbook "all or nothing" rule has no way to say this.
- A recursive "finish a step, then decide its followers" overflows the Python stack when a failure cascades along a chain; ours uses a queue. Test: `test_a_long_failure_cascade_does_not_recurse` (3,000 steps).
- A waiting step must not hold a worker, or one approval would block a whole pool: `test_a_waiting_step_releases_its_slot`.
- FIFO is the obvious queue and is wrong when a short step opens a long tail: `test_longest_path_beats_fifo_when_a_short_step_opens_a_long_tail` (makespan 12 with FIFO, 11 with the longest path).

## Pseudocode

See the Project Proposal, section 6 (C3). In short:

```text
start:    queue every step with no predecessors
decide(i) when all predecessors of i are terminal:
              verdict = join_verdict(i.join, states of predecessors)
              if verdict: finish(i, verdict)
              elif i.when is false: finish(i, SKIPPED)         # an error in `when` finishes FAILED
              else: push i on the ready heap with key (-longest_chain(i), declaration index)
advance:  while the queue is not empty: decide(pop); then
          while the heap is not empty and running < m: pop and emit Start(i, action, rendered args)
finish(i): set the state, log the event, decrement the count of every follower, queue the ones at 0;
           if i FAILED and i.on_failure = fail: cancel everything that is not terminal
the run ends when every step is terminal; outcome from the goals, or FAILED / CANCELLED
```

## Complexity

Every step is decided once and every edge is counted once, each ready step goes through the heap
once: O((n + e) log n) for a plan of n steps and e dependencies. Priorities are computed once at
the start in O(n + e) (the plan is in topological order). A fan-in reads its instances once per
reader, O(instances). Memory is O(n + e). Measured: the test with a failure cascade over 3,000 steps takes 0.8 s including the compilation of
the workflow; a simulated run of 200 random steps takes about 1.2 ms (scheduler and virtual clock).

## Oracle (independent check)

`tests/unit/engine/test_properties.py`: an independent, naive evaluator of the same semantics (it
recomputes the state of every step by repeated passes over the plan, with no queue, no heap and no
times) is compared with the scheduler on random workflows (random DAGs, random `when`, join rules,
failures and failure policies) for 1, 2, 3 and 100 workers: final states, outcome, outputs and
errors. The same file checks the invariants (no step starts before its predecessors are terminal,
at most `m` running, every step terminal at the end), that the same seed gives the same history,
that with enough workers the makespan equals the longest chain, and that one worker runs one step
at a time.

## Measurement (level 1)

`benchmarks/bench_priority.py` and `docs/research/priority-rules.md`: on 600 random DAGs the plain
longest path is within 0.4% of the lower bound; FIFO is up to 21.5% above it. The longest path
was shorter in 534 cases, equal in 63 and longer in 3.

## Level 2: the feasible critical path

`analysis/critical.py` (about 150 lines), dsl-spec 11. The scheduler takes the priorities as an
argument (`Scheduler(..., priorities=...)`, also `simulate`); the run service computes them for
the inputs of the run when it launches or recovers a run. The plain longest path stays as the
default and as the baseline.

**Borrowed:** the longest path in a DAG by dynamic programming over a topological order (the same
idea as the level of a node above).

**Ours:** the plain recurrence adds the durations along edges and so counts a chain whose guards
cannot hold in the same run. We keep, for each step, up to 16 *alternatives*: pairs (length,
condition) of chains that start at it, where the condition is the conjunction of the run
conditions (C2, `analysis/runs.py`) of the chain's steps. A step meets the alternatives of all its
descendants, not only its successors, which lets a chain pass over a step that was skipped (the
join `all_done`); an alternative is kept only when the solver (C6) does not prove its condition
unsatisfiable. With the inputs of the run known, their values are put into the guards first
(`restrict`), so most conditions are decided without the solver.

```text
for step in reverse topological order:
    candidates = [(d(step), ran(step))] +
                 [(d(step) + L, ran(step) and C) for D in descendants(step) for (L, C) in alts[D]]
    sort by length, longest first
    keep the first 16 whose condition is not UNSAT (a condition equal to one already kept is skipped);
    if more are left, or 128 queries were spent: add the longest left out under ran(step), mark a cut
    priority(step) = length of the first kept
```

**Where the plain version is wrong:** the example of the Proposal (A 1 s, B 10 s when x > 5, C 10 s
when x < 3 after B with `all_done`): the plain path of A is 21 s, no execution has a chain longer
than 11 s, the feasible priority is 11 (`test_the_example_of_the_proposal_is_11_and_not_21`). A
chain whose steps need `decision == 'approved'` and `decision == 'rejected'` is dropped
(`test_a_chain_is_not_counted_across_branches_that_exclude_each_other`). The story US-35 is the
same situation in a workflow (`tests/acceptance/test_us35.py`).

**Error direction:** every priority is an upper bound of the chain that really runs, and the
plain longest path is an upper bound of the priority. An atom the solver cannot read counts as
possible, a condition over 300 nodes is replaced by the one of the first step, and a cut keeps the
longest chain left out under a weaker condition. Dropping chains instead (a lower bound) was the
first version; the benchmark showed that it made the schedule worse than the plain path (1.088
against 1.006 of the bound, 200 steps and 4 workers, no inputs), because 197 of 200 workflows hit
the cap.

**Complexity:** a step meets O(n) descendants with up to 16 alternatives each, sorts them and
spends at most 128 solver queries: O(n^2 log n) candidates in the worst case, bounded in queries
by 128 n. Measured below.

**Oracle:** `tests/unit/analysis/test_critical.py`, against the reference interpreter. With every
input known and no failures, the priority of every step that runs is exactly the longest chain of
steps that ran from it (about 5,700 comparisons over four seeds, and the largest priority against
the interpreter's `longest_chain`); with the inputs unknown, the priority is at least the chain of
every execution (about 8,000 checks) and at most the plain path. Four faults fail it: all chains declared
feasible, the inputs not put into the guards, only direct successors considered, opaque atoms read
as false.

**Measurement:** `benchmarks/bench_critical.py`, `docs/research/priority-rules.md`.

**Limits:** the cap bites almost always without the inputs (97 to 100 of 100 random workflows of
50 or 200 steps), so without them the result is mostly the plain path with some pruning; with the
inputs of the run the conditions are decided and the cost is 0.5 to 4 ms. Durations are the true
ones in the benchmark and 1 in a real run (no history of runs yet). Failures are not modelled:
the conditions are those of an optimistic run.

## Stated limits

- Priority is a heuristic (list scheduling with limited workers is NP-hard); it is not optimal and it is not always better than FIFO.
- The plain priority counts every chain as if it would run. The feasible critical path (level 2, above) is the answer to this; it gains little on random workflows (at most 0.9% of the makespan on average) and its cost is in the research note.
- `retry` and the timeout of a waiting step are not applied in M2 (they belong to C4 and M3). A cancelled step whose thread is still running is not interrupted; its late result is ignored.
- The scheduler is single-threaded by design: the cost of one decision is small compared with an action (reading a file, a database write), so the driver thread is not a bottleneck at the sizes of the Committee's workflows (tens of steps).
