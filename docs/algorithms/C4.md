# C4. Fault tolerance

Increment: I3. Finished in M3 (5 November 2026).

## Two levels

- **Level 1 (known algorithm, our transformation):** Write-ahead log and replay: an event is written before it is acknowledged; a step caught in RUNNING is re-run if idempotent, otherwise UNCERTAIN.
- **Level 2 (built on the output of level 1):** Saga compensation plan: compensations of completed steps in reverse dependency order, resumable after a crash during compensation.
- **Dependence:** Level 2 is built from the replayed state of level 1: it must know which steps really completed, including UNCERTAIN ones.
- **Baseline for the benchmark:** Restart from scratch and naive reverse order, against ours: duplicated side effects in the crash-at-every-event suite.

## Problem

An engine process can stop at any instant: killed, out of memory, power lost. The run it was driving must not be lost, a step that had finished must not be done again, and a step that was in flight must not be done a second time if the second time would do harm. Failures that do not stop the engine matter too: a transient error is retried, a failed run is undone in an order that respects what depends on what, and a call whose answer was lost must not make its effect twice.

## Why a library does not solve it

Workflow engines that do this (Temporal, Airflow, Celery with a result backend) are services with a server, a database and a model of their own; the engine here has to be a small library of standard-library code (`CLAUDE.md`), with a history that is one SQLite table and a scheduler that is a pure function of events. They also stop at "at least once": a step that was running is run again. The Committee's workflows contain steps (sending a mail) where a second run is harm, so the case that matters, the step that may have taken effect, needs a state of its own and a person's decision, which a retry policy cannot give.

## What is borrowed (textbook algorithms, named)

- Write-ahead logging: the record of a change is stored before the change is made (the principle of ARIES-style recovery, without pages or undo logs: here the log is the only state).
- Event sourcing: the state of a run is a fold over its events; the report (A2) and the restore are two folds over the same stored history.
- Sagas (Garcia-Molina and Salem, 1987): a long transaction as steps with compensations, undone in reverse order of dependency.
- Idempotency keys (the technique of payment APIs): the receiver stores the answer under the key and answers a repeat from the store.
- Transitive closure by bitsets in reverse topological order (descendants of every node in one pass).

## What is ours (the transformation and the code we write)

- `recovery/wal.py` (about 30 lines): the events of one call into the scheduler are stored in one transaction, with `synchronous = FULL`, before the commands of that call are dispatched (`engine/runner.py`, argument `journal`).
- `Scheduler.restore` (`engine/scheduler.py`, about 180 lines with the fold and the helpers): folds the recorded events into the states, then derives everything else from the states (counters of unfinished predecessors, the ready queue, the bookkeeping of the compensation phase); re-renders the arguments of the steps that ran from the recorded outputs; completes a stop that was recorded in part; decides again only steps that were decided but had no event of their own.
- The rule for a call in flight (`_recover`): idempotent action: the same call again with the same attempt number; any other: the step becomes UNCERTAIN (E-REC-001), nothing is run, steps that depend on it wait, independent steps go on, the run waits for a person.
- `Scheduler.resolve` and the commands `resume` and `resolve`: the person's decision, `done` (with the output) or `retry` (a new attempt), recorded as events. The same rule holds for an undo that was in flight.
- `recovery/saga.py` (about 50 lines): for the steps to undo, the steps that must be undone first, through every chain of `needs` (also through steps that have no compensation), by one bitset per step. The compensation phase in the scheduler runs the undo calls from this plan with the same slots, retries and events as the steps themselves (`_begin_undo`, `_undo_dispatch`, `_undo_finished`).
- `actions/http.py` and the key of the operation: a call to another system sends `run/step` (the same for every attempt) so that a retry after a lost answer is recognised.
- The crash-injection suite (`tests/crash/`) and the fault-injecting receiver (`tests/support/fault_server.py`).

## Adaptation: where the plain textbook version is wrong

- **Plain replay repeats a call that may already have taken effect.** The textbook recovery re-runs what was in flight. For a mail that is a second mail. Ours turns the step into UNCERTAIN. Test: `test_the_rejected_example_never_repeats_the_mail_after_a_crash` (a crash after every event of the rejected example) and `test_us16_a_step_that_is_not_idempotent_becomes_uncertain_and_nothing_is_run_again`; with `email.send` registered as idempotent (the plain version, checked by changing the flag), 7 tests fail in the crash and acceptance suites.
- **Restart from scratch repeats finished work and every effect.** Measured in `research/recovery-measurement.md`: 12 repeated calls per crash point on the example against 2.6 for ours, and 432 of the 550 are work that was finished.
- **An attempt number in the key breaks the retry after a lost answer.** The key `run/step/attempt` is new for the retry, so the receiver would make the effect again. `http.call` sends the key of the operation. Test: `test_the_effect_exists_exactly_once` (six scripts of faults); `test_a_receiver_that_ignores_the_key_gets_the_effect_twice` fixes the limit.
- **An order that looks only at direct dependencies undoes a step too early** when the dependency runs through a step that has no compensation (a, then x without an undo, then b: undoing b must come first). Test: `test_a_step_without_compensation_stays_succeeded_but_still_orders_the_others`; 43 tests fail across the suite, among them the comparison with the naive search on random DAGs, when the planner is changed to look at direct edges only (checked by changing the code). Measured: 78 of 1,466 undo calls too early.
- **A stop recorded in part.** A failure stored and the cancellations of the other steps not: a restore that trusts the states would run the cancelled steps again. Ours completes the stop. Test: `test_a_stop_that_was_recorded_only_in_part_is_completed`; the crash suite cuts after every single event, so it covers the case although one call is one transaction.
- **Compensation after a crash.** An undo that was finished is not repeated, one that was in flight follows the rule above, one that failed keeps the steps behind it as they are. Tests in `test_restore.py` and the crash after every event of a saga, with a failing undo and with parallel undos.

## Pseudocode

```text
commit(events):        one transaction: store the events that are not stored yet   (before dispatch)

restore(history, now, idempotent):
    states, outputs, attempts <- fold of the recorded events
    if the run has an outcome: return nothing
    log RUN_RESUMED
    counters <- number of predecessors that are not terminal, for every step
    stopped, cancelled, compensate_requested <- read from the states (FAILED with a stopping policy, CANCELLED)
    args[i] <- render(with of step i) from the recorded outputs, for every step that ran
    if stopped and not undoing: cancel everything that is not terminal      # completes a partial stop
    for every step in RUNNING (or COMPENSATING):
        if idempotent(its action): command Start(same attempt)              # same key
        else: state UNCERTAIN, log STEP_UNCERTAIN(E-REC-001)
    WAITING stays WAITING; UNCERTAIN stays UNCERTAIN
    if undoing: rebuild the blockers from the states and dispatch the undo calls that are ready
    else: queue every PENDING step with no unfinished predecessor and advance the scheduler

resolve(step, decision, output):       only for UNCERTAIN
    done:  log STEP_RESOLVED; finish the step (an undo: COMPENSATED) through the ordinary path
    retry: attempt + 1; log STEP_RESOLVED; command Start(new attempt)

blockers(plan, to_undo):               bitset of descendants, reverse topological order
    below[i] <- OR over followers j of i of (bit j OR below[j])
    return {s: below[s] AND to_undo for s in to_undo}
undo order: a step waits until the undo of all its blockers is COMPENSATED; later steps first among the ready ones
```

## Complexity

The write-ahead log stores each event once: O(events) in total, one transaction per call into the scheduler. The restore is O(events) for the fold plus O(V + E) for the counters and the queue plus the cost of rendering arguments, plus the planner when the run was compensating. The planner builds one integer bitset per step in one pass over the plan, O(V (V + E) / w) for words of w bits, and a membership test is a mask. Memory is O(V + E) and O(events) for the history.

## Oracle (independent check)

The crash-injection suite (`tests/crash/test_every_event.py`, 170 tests) compares every restored run with the uninterrupted run, and the uninterrupted run is produced by the same scheduler code, so it checks that a crash changes nothing, not that the scheduler is right; the scheduler is checked by the independent evaluator of C3 (`tests/unit/engine/test_properties.py`). What it compares after a crash at every event of the nine-region example (approved and rejected), of a saga with parallel undos, with a failing undo, and of 20 random sagas, and after two crashes in a row: the outcome, the state and the output of every step, the keys of all calls, that no finished step is called again, that the sequence of events continues without a gap, that the report folded from the events agrees with the scheduler, and that every undo follows the undos of everything that depends on its step. The planner of the order is compared with a naive search (a depth-first walk from every step) on 40 random DAGs (`tests/unit/recovery/test_saga.py`). Three deliberate faults put into the restore (the next attempt number, no repeated call, no completion of a partial stop) make the suite fail (109, 110 and 14 failing tests).

## Measurement

`benchmarks/bench_recovery.py`, `benchmarks/bench_wal.py`, `research/recovery-measurement.md`. After a crash at every event of the nine-region example, going on from the history repeats 2.6 calls per crash point and restart from scratch 12; on random workloads with a fifth of the steps sending mail, plain replay would repeat a mail whose first call may have worked 898 times over 2,040 crash points and ours never, at the price of asking a person 898 times. Reverse plan order is 2.6 times slower than ours; direct dependencies only is 4% faster and wrong in 5.3% of the undo calls. Storing the history first costs 0.87 ms per call into the scheduler on this machine (19 ms for the example), ten times the cost without the flush to disk.

## Stated limits

- Exactly-once is not possible without the receiver. The guarantee is: an idempotent action is repeated with the same key; any other is never repeated automatically and a person decides. `http.call` is registered as idempotent on the promise that the receiver honours the key; with a receiver that ignores it the effect can be made twice (a test shows it).
- The fault model is a stop of the engine and of its calls, one process per run. A call that is still running in another process when the engine restarts is not seen; the switch of the owner of a run is C5 (M8).
- A step that was cancelled while running may have had an effect that is not recorded and is not undone.
- A clock jump is not part of the fault model. The restore starts a repeated call at once, without the rest of a retry delay.
- An operator's `retry` of an UNCERTAIN step starts at once and may exceed the limit of parallel steps by one call per decision.
- `resume` takes up the run that is named; a process that finds and resumes every unfinished run at start-up is part of the deployment (M8).
- The effects are modelled in the measurement (a started step may have taken effect); the numbers are from one machine and one distribution of workflows.
